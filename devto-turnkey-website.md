# Turnkey Website Development: What You Actually Get for Your Money

"Turnkey" is the most abused word in web development. It promises a finished result and usually delivers a template with your logo on it.

Here is what a genuinely turnkey project looks like — what you should receive, what it costs, and the three questions that separate a real developer from a reseller.

## What "turnkey" actually means

A turnkey project is one where the client hands over a key and starts using it. That means someone else has dealt with:

- Hosting and DNS, with SSL working from day one
- A content structure that matches how the business actually sells
- Analytics, Search Console and a call-tracking pixel — installed, not "instructions in the email"
- A form that delivers leads somewhere you actually check
- Mobile layout that isn't a scaled-down desktop version
- The deployment pipeline, so updates don't break the site

If any of those are still on the client's to-do list, the project is not turnkey. It's a build.

If a quote for a 20-page corporate site comes in at $300, someone is going to hit a wall at the first non-trivial request. The price is a signal of how many decisions were actually made.

## The 5-day version, step by step

Most clients ask for speed, so here's how a 5-day business website actually runs.

**Day 1 — brief, structure, content.** We map the pages, agree on what each page must make a visitor do, and collect the text. If the copy isn't ready by end of day 1, the deadline moves. This single fact is the most common reason a "5-day" project becomes a three-week project.

**Day 2 — design.** Wireframes, then visual design, then approval. One round of revisions is included; five rounds adds three days.

**Day 3 — development.** HTML/CSS, CMS setup, forms, analytics, tracking pixel. The boring part that takes 60% of the time.

**Day 4 — content and SEO.** Text placed, meta tags written, images optimized, structured data added, sitemap generated. This is where most rushed projects cut corners — and why they rank on page 5 instead of page 1.

**Day 5 — testing and launch.** Cross-browser, mobile, speed test, DNS switch, SSL. It goes live.

## What you should receive, in writing

Ask any developer for these seven items before paying the final invoice:

1. **The source code** — in a repository you own, not a zip from a contractor's laptop
2. **Domain and hosting accounts** — registered in your name, with access handed over
3. **A deployment pipeline** — so the next update is a pull request, not a panicked FTP upload
4. **Documentation** — how to edit text, add a page, publish a post
5. **Analytics and Search Console access** — in your account, not theirs
6. **A list of what was deliberately not done** — the honest version of scope
7. **30 days of warranty fixes** — for the things that break after launch, which always happen

Item 6 is the one nobody asks for and the one that saves the most money. A contractor who clearly states "form validation is not implemented, here's how to add it" is more reliable than one who says everything is done.

## The three questions that reveal everything

**"What happens when I need to change the prices on 40 pages?"** — If the answer is "you edit them in the admin panel," you have a CMS. If it's "we'll do it for you," you're locked in.

**"Who owns the code and the accounts?"** — Everything. Domain, hosting, repository, CMS, analytics. If any of it sits with the developer, that's a dependency and a risk.

**"Show me a project you finished six months ago that's still live."** — A site from two years ago says more about quality than any portfolio screenshot.

## What to avoid

- **Templates rebranded.** If the demo looks suspiciously like another client's site, that's what you'll get.
- **Monthly retainers for a site that doesn't change.** If the site doesn't need updates, hosting and a maintenance window are enough.
- **Work without a written scope.** Every dispute in this industry comes from an unclear agreement about what was included.
- **Publishing before testing on real devices.** Half your traffic is on a phone; a broken mobile layout costs you the leads you paid for.

## A simple way to start

If you're not sure what you need, describe the process a customer goes through right now — from first contact to a finished order. That single walkthrough usually tells you whether you need a landing page, a five-page site, or a store with automation behind it.

Then take the cheapest option that solves that process. You can always add pages later; you can't easily un-spend a bad architecture decision.
